Shares are bought and sold in two different "places", and the difference matters because it decides who receives your money.

Primary marketSecondary market
What happensA company issues new sharesInvestors trade existing shares among themselves
Who gets your moneyThe company (or selling shareholders in an offer-for-sale)The investor who sold to you
ExamplesIPO, FPO, rights issueEveryday buying and selling on NSE/BSE
PriceFixed or set within a price bandChanges continuously during market hours

Why the secondary market matters to companies

A company raises money only in the primary market. But investors are willing to buy in an IPO partly because they know they can later sell on the secondary market. This liquidity is what makes listed shares attractive.

Types of primary issues

  • IPO (Initial Public Offering): the first time a company offers shares to the public (see the IPO chapter).
  • FPO (Follow-on Public Offer): a listed company issues more shares to the public.
  • Rights issue: existing shareholders get the right to buy new shares, usually at a discount, in proportion to what they already hold.
  • Offer for Sale (OFS): existing shareholders (often promoters) sell their shares to the public; the company itself receives nothing.

Key takeaways

  • Primary market = new shares, money goes to the issuer.
  • Secondary market = existing shares, money goes to another investor.
  • In an OFS, the company receives no money.
