Ratios turn financial statements into comparable numbers. Three of the most widely used are below, calculated for our fictional Company A (PAT ₹112 cr, PBT ₹150 cr, interest ₹30 cr, equity ₹600 cr, debt ₹250 cr).

RatioFormulaCompany AWhat it tells you
ROE: Return on EquityPAT ÷ Shareholders' equity112 ÷ 600 = 18.7%Profit generated on the shareholders' money
ROCE: Return on Capital EmployedEBIT ÷ (Equity + Debt)(150 + 30) ÷ 850 = 21.2%Profit generated on all long-term capital, owned and borrowed
Debt-to-equityTotal debt ÷ Equity250 ÷ 600 = 0.42How much the company relies on borrowing

Reading them together

  • A high ROE achieved with low debt generally reflects a stronger business than the same ROE achieved with heavy borrowing, because debt boosts ROE but adds risk.
  • ROCE helps compare companies with different debt levels.
  • Compare against the company's own history and its industry peers. Banks, for example, naturally carry high leverage, so these ratios read differently there.

Limitations

  • Ratios use accounting numbers, which involve estimates.
  • One year can be unusual, so look at 5–10 year averages.
  • A ratio is a question to investigate, not a verdict.

Key takeaways

  • ROE = return on shareholders' money; ROCE = return on all capital.
  • Debt can inflate ROE while raising risk.
  • Always compare with history and peers.
