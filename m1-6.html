An Initial Public Offering is when a company sells shares to the public for the first time and then lists on a stock exchange.

The journey

StepWhat happens
1. DRHPThe company files a Draft Red Herring Prospectus with SEBI. It describes the business, financials, risks and how the money will be used.
2. SEBI observationsSEBI reviews the draft and may ask for changes.
3. RHP and price bandThe final offer document and a price band (for example ₹95–₹100) are announced.
4. BiddingThe issue stays open for a few working days. Investors bid in multiples of a fixed lot.
5. AllotmentThe registrar finalises who gets shares.
6. ListingShares must list within three working days of the issue closing (the "T+3" timeline, mandatory since December 2023).

Investor categories

  • Retail individual investors: apply for up to ₹2 lakh.
  • Non-institutional investors (NII/HNI): above ₹2 lakh.
  • Qualified institutional buyers (QIB): mutual funds, insurers, foreign portfolio investors and similar.

Applying

Applications are made through ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount). Your money stays in your bank account and is only blocked, then debited if you get shares. Individuals can apply through UPI for bids up to ₹5 lakh. If you don't get an allotment, the block is released.

When an IPO is oversubscribed

If retail demand exceeds the shares available, allotment is by lottery, aiming to give as many applicants as possible at least one lot. Many applicants get nothing.

Reading an offer document

Focus on: the risk factors section, objects of the issue (fresh money for growth, or existing holders cashing out?), financial history, debt, pending litigation, and related-party transactions. A popular IPO is not automatically a good business, and listing-day prices can fall as well as rise.

Key takeaways

  • An IPO is a company's first sale of shares to the public.
  • Money is only blocked (ASBA) until allotment.
  • Read the risk factors and objects of the issue, not just the hype.
